The Checklist for Buying Water

The Trust's own screening criteria for water deals are thorough on legal and physical questions — and silent on who's on the other side of the table.

Posted Sept. 9, 2026 · Page look updated Nov. 2, 2026

Document: Criteria for Evaluating Water Transaction Opportunities
Source: Great Salt Lake Watershed Enhancement Trust

The short version

Before the Great Salt Lake Watershed Enhancement Trust spends money buying or leasing water for the lake, it runs each potential deal through a written checklist — seven things a deal must pass, and eight more that make a deal more attractive.

The checklist is genuinely thorough on the legal and physical questions: does the seller actually own clear title, can the water legally reach the lake, is the price in a reasonable range. What it never asks, anywhere in the document, is whether the person or company on the other side of the transaction has a connection to anyone reviewing or approving the deal.

That gap matters more here than it would somewhere else, because this project has already documented that the body reviewing these transactions — the Trust Council — includes members with direct industry ties to Great Salt Lake water and mineral interests, some of whom sit on more than one of the lake's governing councils at once.

  • What it is: The Trust's own written framework for screening water-transaction opportunities before they're proposed as Major Expenditures.
  • What it measures well: Legal ownership, physical deliverability, monitoring ability, and cost-effectiveness relative to comparable deals.
  • What it doesn't ask: Whether a seller or counterparty is connected to a Trust Council member, the Commissioner, or another official with a role in approving the deal.
  • What's built in on purpose: An explicit flexibility clause that lets evaluators set aside the checklist for a "unique or creative" opportunity.

01

The Checklist Itself

The Trust's evaluation framework runs on two tiers. Threshold criteria are pass/fail — a deal that fails one is out, at least in theory: clear title and ownership, evidence of legal historic use, a legal pathway to transfer the right to Great Salt Lake use, a physical pathway to actually deliver the water, proof the deal creates real net new inflow (not water that was already surplus or "stacked"), the ability to monitor the benefit once it's done, and cost-effectiveness against comparable transactions.

Desired criteria don't disqualify a deal but make it more attractive: whether it builds a partnership useful for future deals, whether the supply is reliable or long-term, whether it's repeatable at scale, how fast and cheap it is to complete, whether it leverages matching or in-kind funding, whether it fits with existing local programs, whether it benefits a priority habitat location, and whether it delivers multiple benefits — water quality, habitat, community, and recreation together.

02

What It Measures Well

Read on its own terms, this is a reasonably rigorous framework. Requiring clear title before spending public and philanthropic money on a water right is basic due diligence, and this project hasn't found evidence it's being skipped. Tying cost-effectiveness to "observed pricing" comparables is a normal way to guard against overpaying, even though it has a real limit worth naming plainly: if the broader market for these water rights is itself inflated, a deal can look reasonable by comparison while still costing more than it should.

The monitoring criterion is worth noting too — the checklist explicitly downgrades transactions where benefits would be hard to verify (dispersed inflow, no equipment in place to measure flow), which is a real acknowledgment that a water deal on paper isn't the same as water reaching the lake.

03

The Flexibility Clause

The document ends with a footnote that applies to the whole framework: flexibility in evaluation is described as important so that unique or creative options aren't excluded.

Worth sitting with

A footnote like this is common in evaluation frameworks and isn't inherently a red flag — rigid checklists can genuinely exclude good, unconventional deals. But it also means every one of the seven "threshold" criteria above is, by the document's own terms, negotiable. Whether that flexibility has actually been used to approve a deal that failed a threshold criterion isn't something this document shows either way.

04

What's Not Asked

Across all 15 threshold and desired criteria, none of them ask a version of: is the seller, lessor, or counterparty connected to anyone who reviews or approves this transaction? Not the Trust Council members, not the Commissioner, not Trust staff.

Two related gaps stand out for the same reason:

  • No public input criterion. Despite "community" appearing as one of several benefits a deal can provide, nothing in the framework asks whether the affected community was consulted or supports the transaction — consistent with what this docket has already documented about the Trust Council's own bylaws, where public participation in meetings is discretionary rather than guaranteed.
  • No explicit water-right seniority weighting. A water right being "too junior to be distributed" appears only as an example negative finding under the physical-pathway threshold, not as its own scored criterion — meaning how much weight priority date actually carries in a real evaluation isn't clear from this document.
05

Why This Connects

This docket's companion piece on the Advisory Council and Trust Council documents that at least two Trust Council members — Timothy Hawkes (Aquaculture) and Joe Havasi (Mineral Extraction) — hold industry-affiliated seats on the Trust Council while also sitting on the public Advisory Council. Neither this evaluation framework nor the Trust Council's own bylaws, as reviewed for that piece, require checking whether a proposed water transaction's counterparty has ties to a reviewing member.

Related Case File

See "One Lake, Two Councils, and One That Calls Itself Private" for the full documentation of dual council membership and the Commissioner's own built-in conflict of interest — a different, already-acknowledged conflict from the one this checklist doesn't screen for at all.

To be clear about what is and isn't established here: this piece has not identified a specific transaction where a Trust Council member had an undisclosed stake in the counterparty. The finding is narrower and still worth having on the record — the written framework used to screen deals doesn't ask the question in the first place.

06

Questions Worth Asking

For readers who want to press further:

  1. Does the Trust disclose, before or after a deal closes, who it's negotiating a water transaction with?
  2. Has any transaction ever been approved despite failing one of the seven threshold criteria, using the framework's own "flexibility" clause?
  3. Should a conflict-of-interest question be added to this checklist, given that Trust Council members hold industry seats?
  4. Who verifies, after a deal closes, that the promised water actually reached the lake?

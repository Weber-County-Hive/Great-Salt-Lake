GSL Fact vs. Fiction: What They Say vs. What the Data Says

A lot gets said about the Great Salt Lake — in press releases, on the campaign trail, in Facebook comments. This is the first entry in an ongoing series holding those claims up against the state's own numbers, one claim at a time. First up: are water diversions actually slowing down, or is Utah drawing more than ever?

New here? This is a companion to Did You Know? The Great Salt Lake, From 1847 to Today and The Life of the Great Salt Lake. Those two cover the basics; this one is a running fact-check log, starting with the single most common claim about the lake's decline.

Claim #1: "They're not really stopping diversions — they're drawing more than ever."

The claim, as it's commonly made

"Despite all the talk about saving the Great Salt Lake, the state hasn't actually reduced how much water gets diverted before it reaches the lake — if anything, more is being taken out than ever before."

Mostly supported — with an important addition

What the data says: Total water depletion from the Great Salt Lake's watershed is up, not down — the Kem C. Gardner Policy Institute's Great Salt Lake Strike Team found total basin-wide water depletion in the early 2020s ran about 5% higher than in the early 1990s. So the core of the claim checks out: diversions have not been reduced overall.

But the "who" has shifted. Agriculture's share of that use actually fell, from about 71% in the 1990s to about 65% in the 2020s — while municipal and industrial use grew from about 24% to about 27% of the total, driven almost entirely by outdoor residential watering (97% of municipal water use in 2024 was outdoor, per the same report). Lawn and landscape watering alone now accounts for roughly 408,500 acre-feet a year — about a quarter of all agricultural water use in the basin, from a single category of municipal use.

The addition worth making: the state has also dramatically scaled up efforts to put water back into the lake in just the last few years — from about 18,000 acre-feet secured in 2021 to 163,468 acre-feet in the most recent year on record. That's a real, documented shift in effort. It just hasn't been enough yet to offset the growth in use elsewhere: the state's own target is at least 800,000 additional acre-feet a year to reach a minimum healthy elevation by 2034 — meaning current gains cover roughly a fifth of what's still needed.

Who's Actually Using the Water

The clearest way to see the shift the data describes is side by side: the 1990s breakdown against the 2020s breakdown of where basin water depletions go.

Share of Basin Water Depletions, By Decade

Hover or tap a bar for sourcing

Figures from the Great Salt Lake Strike Team's 2026 Data and Insights Summary, comparing the first four years of each decade. Remaining share in both decades goes to mineral extraction, impounded wetlands, and reservoir evaporation.

  • Indoor municipal water use has stayed roughly flat even as the population has grown — the increase is almost entirely outdoor watering.
  • Per-resident water use has mostly declined since 1989 due to conservation, but total municipal use has still risen because there are simply more residents.
  • None of this erases agriculture's role — it's still the largest single water user in the basin by a wide margin. The finding is that it's no longer the whole story, and hasn't been for a while.

More Claims to Check

This series will grow as more claims come up. On deck, pending sourcing:

  • "The Bear River Development pipeline would only cost the lake a small amount of water." The one specific project figure in the academic literature (Wurtsbaugh et al., 2016) put a completed Bear River pipeline at an additional 8.5-inch elevation drop and roughly 30 more square miles of exposed lakebed — worth checking against the project's current status and any newer estimates before treating either number as final.
  • "Mineral extraction is a bigger water user than agriculture." Not supported by current data — mineral extractors account for roughly 7% of basin depletions combined, far behind agriculture's roughly two-thirds share (see the companion piece's mineral-extraction section for the named companies and figures).
